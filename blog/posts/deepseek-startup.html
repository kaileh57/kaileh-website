DeepSeek: The Chinese Startup That Made Sam Altman Sweat

January 24, 2025

Last week, the App Store rankings witnessed a stunning upset. ChatGPT, the undisputed champion since its debut, found itself dethroned by an unlikely challenger: DeepSeek, an app created by a relatively unknown Chinese AI startup.

To grasp the magnitude of this event, picture someone outselling Taylor Swift's latest album. It's unheard of. Yet, there it was, a Chinese app with a quirky logo and less-than-perfect English description, outperforming the catalyst of the AI revolution.

But DeepSeek is no mere ChatGPT imitator. It upends the foundations of what we believed about AI development. Silicon Valley has long asserted that China's AI progress is hindered by restricted access to advanced chips. They're confined to outdated tech while we sprint ahead with the latest Nvidia hardware. They might imitate our breakthroughs, but they'll perpetually lag behind.

DeepSeek demolished that narrative.

Their crown jewel, the DeepSeek R1 model, doesn't merely rival OpenAI's latest reasoning model, it surpasses it in certain benchmarks. The real bombshell? They offer it at a 97% discount. Not 10%, not 50%, but a staggering ninety-seven percent. If ChatGPT is a lavish $20 restaurant burger, DeepSeek serves up the identical burger from a food truck for a mere sixty cents.

The figures are mind-boggling. DeepSeek claims a paltry $6 million for R1's final training run, while OpenAI splurged an estimated $100 million on GPT-4's full development. This isn't a minor discrepancy or clever accounting. It's a radical divergence in methodology that exposes Silicon Valley's "bigger is better" mindset as wasteful and antiquated.

So, what's their secret sauce? Ingenious engineering played a significant role. As American companies hurled money and computing power at the problem, DeepSeek innovated. They pioneered novel training techniques, streamlined their algorithms, and extracted maximum performance from their limited hardware. Picture someone clinching a Formula 1 race in a tricked-out Toyota Prius.

But the real innovation is their business model, or the absence of one. DeepSeek is fully open source. Anyone can download the complete model, examine its inner workings, modify it, and build upon it. For free. This defies the norms of AI companies, who typically guard their model weights like state secrets, charge subscription fees, and fortify their tech with moats.

The AI establishment's response has been intriguing. OpenAI insinuated improper data harvesting but provided no proof. Industry insiders mutter about Chinese government subsidies, as if that explains how a hedge fund side project embarrassed companies worth hundreds of billions. The cope is real.

What's particularly humiliating for the China hawks is that we forced them into it. Those chip export controls designed to cripple Chinese AI development? They merely spurred Chinese companies to get inventive. We tried to starve them out but inadvertently catalyzed an agricultural revolution.

User reviews reveal the truth. "Works just as well as ChatGPT but doesn't lecture me." "Finally, an AI that just answers questions." "Why was I paying $20 a month for this?" DeepSeek eliminated much of the safety theater and corporate jargon that makes ChatGPT feel like conversing with HR. Users adore it.

For AI race spectators, DeepSeek signifies more than just another chatbot. It proves that the AI future won't be dictated by the deepest pockets or the fanciest chips, but by the brightest ideas. And those ideas can originate anywhere.

The geopolitical ramifications are immense. If a small Chinese company can equal OpenAI on a shoestring budget, imagine what happens when the Chinese government gets serious. What happens when every nation realizes they don't need Silicon Valley's blessing to develop advanced AI?

There's also a delightful irony at play. Silicon Valley has long preached disruption, moving fast and breaking things, thinking differently. Then a Chinese startup embodies those very principles, and suddenly everyone cries foul. Turns out disruption isn't as fun when you're the disrupted.

But beyond the memes and the market moves, DeepSeek changes how technological competition works. The old arrangement, where a handful of companies in a few countries owned the future, is falling apart. When breakthrough AI can be built by a hedge fund side project in Hangzhou, we're in uncharted territory.

For students like me, this is both thrilling and daunting. Thrilling because the tools of tomorrow are becoming accessible to all. Daunting because it means global competition for everything (jobs, opportunities, attention) just intensified like never before.

DeepSeek may not hold its number one spot. Apple has a knack for featuring apps that don't compete with its partners. But the cat's out of the bag. They've demonstrated that you don't need Silicon Valley's resources to build Silicon Valley-caliber AI. You just need to be clever, resourceful, and willing to share.

Sam Altman recently tweeted that AGI might demand $7 trillion in infrastructure investment. But honestly? It'll probably be open source.
