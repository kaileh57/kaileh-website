DeepSeek: The Chinese Startup That Made Sam Altman Sweat

January 24, 2025

What happened last week in the App Store charts was shocking.

The App Store champ since its launch - ChatGPT - was beaten.

It was beaten by an unlikely challenger, a Chinese AI app (DeepSeek) from a relatively unknown Chinese AI startup.

Just imagine this - it's like Taylor Swift's latest album being outsold by someone else.

It's unheard of.

And yet, there was a Chinese app, with some weird logo and a less-than-perfect English description, beating the AI revolution catalyst.

And DeepSeek isn't just some ChatGPT copycat app.

They're changing everything we thought we knew about AI development.

Silicon Valley has been telling us for years that China's AI development is limited because they can't get their hands on advanced chips.

They're stuck with old tech and we're running with the latest Nvidia chips.

They may copy what we do but they'll always be behind.

DeepSeek shattered that myth.

Their best model (DeepSeek R1) doesn't just compete with the latest OpenAI reasoning model.

It beats it in some benchmarks.

The real game changer - they're offering it 97% off.

Not 10%.

Not even 50%.

97% off.

If ChatGPT is a fancy $20 restaurant burger, they're serving the same burger from a food truck for just 60 cents.

And the figures are mind-boggling.

DeepSeek says they used just $6 million for the final training run of R1.

OpenAI spent an estimated $100 million to develop GPT-4 fully.

It's not just some small difference or accounting trick.

It's a completely different approach.

It shows how wasteful and outdated Silicon Valley "bigger is better" thinking is.

Okay, how'd they do it? Well, part of it was some nifty engineering. While American companies threw money and computing power at it, DeepSeek got creative. They developed new training methods. They optimized their algorithms. They squeezed every bit of performance out of their limited hardware. It's like someone winning a Formula 1 race with a tricked-out Toyota Prius.

But their real magic is in their business model, or lack thereof. They're fully open source. You can download the whole model and see what makes it tick. Tinker with it. Expand on it.

All for free.

That's the opposite of how AI companies usually operate. They keep those model weights locked down like state secrets. Want to use them? Pay a subscription. And they protect their tech with moats.

The reaction from the AI world has been interesting. OpenAI hinted they were harvesting data improperly, but provided no evidence. People in the industry are whispering about Chinese government funding. As if that explains how a hedge fund side project embarrassed companies worth hundreds of billions.

The cope is real.

The real kicker for all the China hawks is we forced them to do it. Those chip export restrictions meant to strangle Chinese AI development? All they did was inspire Chinese companies to get creative. We tried to starve them. Instead we kicked off an agricultural revolution.

User reviews don't lie. "Works just as well as ChatGPT but doesn't lecture me." "Finally, an AI that just answers questions." "Why was I paying $20 a month for this?" DeepSeek cut out a lot of the safety theater and corporate speak that makes ChatGPT feel like talking to HR. People love it.

DeepSeek is not just another chatbot for the spectators of the AI race. It means AI is not a game for those with the deepest pockets. Or the best chips. It's a game for the brightest ideas. And those ideas can come from anywhere.

The geopolitical implications are massive. If it's possible for a scrappy Chinese startup to match OpenAI on a shoestring budget, think about what happens if the Chinese government starts taking this seriously. Think about what happens when countries around the world understand that they don't have to get permission from Silicon Valley to create advanced AI.

The irony is delicious. Silicon Valley's been all about disruption, move fast and break things, think different. Then you have a Chinese startup that is a perfect embodiment of these values. And now everyone's up in arms. Guess disrupting isn't that fun when you're on the receiving end.

DeepSeek isn't just about memes and market reactions. It's about changing the game when it comes to technological competition. The old model where a few companies in a few countries hold the key to the future? It's unraveling. When you can get breakthrough AI out of a hedge fund's side project in Hangzhou, we're in new territory.

As a student, it's both exhilarating and daunting. Exhilarating because the future tools are coming within everyone's reach. Daunting because that means global competition for everything (jobs, opportunities, attention) just got ratcheted up to a whole new level.

DeepSeek may not stay on top. Apple's good at putting apps in their spotlight that don't compete with their partners. But the genie's out of the bottle now. They've shown that it's possible to create Silicon Valley-level AI without Silicon Valley's resources. You just have to be clever, resourceful and willing to share.

Sam Altman recently tweeted that we may need to spend $7 trillion in infrastructure for AGI. But to be frank: it's probably going to be open source.
