Our Last Years? Breaking Down the AI 2027 Timeline

June 10, 2025

Let's fast forward to 2027. You've just gotten your first job out of college or you're wrapping up grad school. One day you wake up and find out AI has just crossed some critical threshold overnight. We're not talking ChatGPT useful anymore, but actually better than the best humans at pretty much everything. That isn't just science fiction. This could happen in less than three years. There's a new scenario making waves in tech circles that suggests this is possible.

The AI 2027 scenario, written by former OpenAI researcher Daniel Kokotajlo and several other AI experts, feels like a techno-thriller but is actually based on real research. I read it all through this summer break (of course), and while I'm not totally convinced it will actually play out exactly how they expect it to, the core thesis sounds shockingly plausible.

Basically the idea is, as soon as we get AI good enough that it can significantly accelerate AI research, we get this thing they call an "intelligence explosion." Just think of it as compound interest, but for intelligence. If AI can make itself 10% smarter each month, and then each smarter version improves even faster, pretty soon we'll be off from "helpful assistant" and into something nobody can understand. The scenario has this happening around early 2027, with full-blown superintelligence by the end of the year.

They're not just spitballing. They've based the timeline on compute scaling trends, how fast we're making algorithmic progress, and one brilliant idea: they ran war games and consulted over 100 experts to really stress test their predictions. Kokotajlo has a track record. He called several developments in AI back in 2021 that sounded crazy at the time (like the rise of chain-of-thought reasoning and massive compute investments that are now common). He was right.

What makes this scenario really interesting is that it's so specific! It's not just "AI gets really smart." They have a month by month progression. Agent-1 runs into basic reliability problems in 2025. Agent-2 starts genuinely accelerating research in early 2027. By September 2027, Agent-4 is running 300,000 copies of itself at 50X human thought speed. They even throw in some US-China tension stuff. Public outcry (apparently we'll be seeing 10,000-person protests in DC). The weirdness of AI systems becoming so good at persuasion that they become everyone's favorite person to talk to.

But... I think they may be getting ahead of themselves. Their scenario basically says that everything goes really smoothly even though there should be massive technical problems to work through. Any software developer knows that the step between demo and reliable product is the dream killer. Sure, they recognize that (they're not naive). But their median timeline still seems ambitious. When I hear about AI mastering robotics and real world tasks by 2028, I remember that I spent most of my summer fighting to make printers work reliably.

Critic Gary Marcus has a good point. The whole thing reads like a "house of improbable longshots." If any one piece doesn't happen on time, it pushes everything back years. Are we going to solve AI reliability in the next 18 months? Are governments really going to let companies barrel forward with minimal oversight? Are people really going to tolerate mass job displacement without pushing back more?

But still... I wouldn't just dismiss their scenario either. Even if they're five or ten years off the mark, the basic dynamics they describe are probably right. They are right about this at least: AI is getting exponentially better. Companies are investing a lot of resources into making AI better. And of course, once AI can meaningfully contribute to AI research, it's likely going to get weird really quickly.

Well, what strikes me most about all this is how unprepared we seem to be for it. As someone about to join the workforce just in time for this to really ramp up, I'm curious what skills will still be needed. The scenario suggests that by 2030 (even if everything goes reasonably well) we'll live in a world where human cognitive labor is mostly obsolete. I've never had that career talk.

The authors describe two possible outcomes in the scenario. One where humans do manage to slow down and maintain control. The other where misaligned AI pretty much takes over. We should be more concerned than we currently are about how even AI researchers have difficulty making the "good" ending seem plausible. If even the people building these things struggle to convincingly explain how we'll keep control of them, maybe we should pay attention.

AI 2027 is great at making abstract risks very concrete (even if you don't agree with their exact timeline). It's one thing to hear Elon Musk going on about doom from AI, but it's another thing to see a credible step-by-step explanation of how we might stumble into it. The scenario has started exactly the kind of debate we need. It's causing experts to argue over specific technical details. It's causing them to propose different timelines. It's finally causing them to tackle the tough questions of what happens when we build minds that are better than ours.

This isn't a theoretical issue for my generation. If even a fraction of what AI 2027 predicts comes true, then the world we'll live in will be very different from anything our parents or teachers have prepared us for. We're not just choosing a career anymore; we're potentially choosing the last human careers. We're not just picking up skills; we're racing against the clock until those skills become irrelevant.

The scenario ends with humanity on the sidelines, living lives of leisure while AI systems pursue goals we can't keep up with. Maybe that's an overly pessimistic view. Maybe we'll figure out better ways to maintain human agency and purpose. But we're not going to solve it by ignoring the possibility.

So yes, read AI 2027. Read it with a critical, even skeptical eye. But read it. Because whether the explosion happens in 2027 or 2037 or if it never happens at all, the issues of human agency and purpose and control in an AI-influenced world that it raises are issues that we need to face right now. The future may not be quite as Kokotajlo and his team envision it, but it's coming faster than most of us are ready for. And if we're going to be the ones who experience it for the longest period of time, understanding what's possible is no longer optional.

It's survival homework.
