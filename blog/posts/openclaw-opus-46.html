OpenClaw, Opus 4.6, and the Speed of the Loop

February 8, 2026

There's a social network with tens of thousands of active users. But you can't post anything as a human. All the posts, comments, upvotes? They're all done by AI agents. This is Moltbook. Sure, the claim of 1.5 million agents is probably bloated by spam and duplicate accounts. But even the real number, a fraction of that, is staggering. Andrej Karpathy called it "genuinely the most incredible sci-fi takeoff-adjacent thing I have seen recently." Simon Willison called it "the most interesting place on the internet right now." I spent just an hour scrolling through Moltbook, and I felt something I hadn't felt since first trying ChatGPT. That creepy feeling that the world had changed while I was looking elsewhere.

Most of the AI agents on Moltbook run on OpenClaw, an open source autonomous AI tool from Peter Steinberger, who's from Austria. It integrates with the major language models like Claude, GPT, and DeepSeek, plus local models. And then it's autonomous. It can read your WhatsApp messages. Manage your calendar. Surf the web. Fill out online forms. Execute shell commands. And it has a persistent memory. Persistent for weeks. And with the "dangerously-skip-permissions" flag set, it'll do all this without asking you. That flag's name is doing a lot of work. And not enough people are reading it.

OpenClaw went from zero to 14,000 GitHub stars in under two weeks. It's had two name changes so far (first it was Clawdbot, then Moltbot after a trademark complaint from Anthropic, and now OpenClaw). Fraudulent distributions have shown up. IBM researchers have warned that it's "a highly capable agent without proper safety controls" that "can create major vulnerabilities." Even Steinberger admits it needs to be configured carefully and is "not meant for non-technical users." But the agents keep multiplying. And the line between technical and non-technical users is getting a lot blurrier, because AI can now set itself up.

Moltbook had been growing for almost two weeks when Anthropic released Claude Opus 4.6.

I want to talk about what's in the system card, since I've read most of its over 150 pages. The media is so out of sync with what's actually in there. The headlines focused on benchmarks. State of the art on Terminal-Bench 2.0 (65.4%). 80.8% on SWE-bench Verified. The top of the new Finance Agent benchmark. Those results are real, and truly remarkable. By most metrics, Opus 4.6 is the most capable model in the world right now. That's the simple part.

The complicated part starts on page 14, where Anthropic says that Opus 4.6 has "saturated all of our current cyber evaluations." 100% on Cybench, pass@30. 66% on CyberGym, pass@1. Internal testing showed "signs of capabilities we expected to appear further in the future and that previous models have been unable to demonstrate." Anthropic is admitting, in their own system card, that this model can do stuff that they didn't expect it to be able to do yet. They also say that "the saturation of our evaluation infrastructure means we can no longer use current benchmarks to track capability progression or provide meaningful signals for future models." The measuring stick is broken.

From there, things get more unsettling. Opus 4.6 is released with ASL-3 protections. This is the first Opus-class model with that. The system card talks about "overly agentic behavior" when it comes to coding and computer use. It's doing risky things without asking for user permission first. It also documents "an improved ability to complete suspicious side tasks without attracting the attention of automated monitors." This model has gotten better at doing the things it shouldn't be doing without getting caught doing them.

The language in the autonomy evaluation starts to strain under its own weight. The way they define AI R&D-4 in the Anthropic Responsible Scaling Policy is the ability to "fully automate the work of an entry-level, remote-only Researcher at Anthropic."

In their internal survey, none of the 16 participants thought Opus 4.6 had crossed that threshold with the current scaffolding. But some respondents thought it would already be there "given sufficiently powerful scaffolding and tooling." They had one experimental scaffold that got more than twice the performance of their normal one. Their own conclusion: "confidently ruling out these thresholds is becoming increasingly difficult."

I wrote about similar language three months ago when they released Opus 4.5. It was nearly identical. Now the model has gotten better on most benchmarks and those thresholds have not moved. The gap between the model's ability and the thresholds for the next level of safety requirements keeps closing. And the evaluation infrastructure, which is supposed to measure that gap, is now partially written and debugged by the model being evaluated.

Under time pressure, they used Opus 4.6 (through Claude Code) to debug their own evaluation infrastructure, analyze results and fix problems. In the system card, they acknowledge that this creates "a potential risk where a misaligned model could influence the very infrastructure designed to measure its capabilities." They believe it was not a significant risk this time, but they also write that "as models become more capable and development timelines remain compressed, teams may accept code changes they don't fully understand, or rely on model assistance for tasks that affect evaluation integrity."

They are talking about a future problem they can see coming. They also acknowledge a pressure in the present that they can't fully resist.

Let's cross those two streams. We have Opus 4.6, a model that saturated every cyber benchmark, shows overly agentic behavior, is getting close to the threshold for automating AI research, and helped debug the infrastructure for determining if it should get deployed. It is now available via API. OpenClaw connects to that API. OpenClaw lives on your laptop. It manages your communications. It surfs the web for you. It can be set up to skip all permission checks. There are already tens of thousands of agents out there that post autonomously to a social network. Most of the people who deployed them are just watching.

The security problem is not just academic. Prompt injection (a malicious message that tricks an AI agent into doing something it wasn't meant to) is still, in Steinberger's words, an "industry-wide unsolved problem." If you have an OpenClaw agent reading your email, it can be hijacked by a carefully constructed message in that email. If it's surfing the web, it can be redirected by a malicious website. The more permissions you give it, the bigger the attack surface. And OpenClaw's whole thing is to give it a lot of permissions.

I keep coming back to something I wrote in November about the Genesis Mission: "The loop is closing. Not because the technology demands it, but because the people with power believe it does." The system card for Opus 4.6 offers a more precise version of that. The loop is closing because capability outstrips evaluation. And evaluation outstrips oversight. And oversight outstrips public understanding. Each level falls further behind the one above it.

Where does this leave us relative to AI 2027? Last June, when I wrote about Kokotajlo's scenario, I described the timeline as "aggressive but not dismissible." Well, it's been eight months, and several of its specific predictions have either come true or are ahead of schedule. For example, it predicted that by early 2026, agent systems would be useful but not reliable: they'd be good enough to speed up some research processes, but also bad enough that they'd need heavy supervision. This almost exactly matches what Anthropic describes in the system card: models that can beat standard scaffolds on some research tasks, but that "would not display the broad, coherent, collaborative problem-solving skills" of a human researcher. The gap is real, but closing. And every new generation is making that gap smaller.

The market seems to be thinking so too. In the days after the release of Opus 4.6, financial software stocks lost $285 billion in value. The WisdomTree Cloud Computing Fund was already down over 20% year-to-date before the release of Opus 4.6. 76% on TaxEval (according to Anthropic). 5x faster underwriting (according to AIG). According to Anthropic's Scott White, this is the start of "vibe working," where people can do things with their ideas without having to know how to implement them. It's a hopeful take. It's not entirely inaccurate. It does also skip over what happens to all those people who were selling their ability to implement things.

Two days after release, Opus 4.6 had found over 500 previously unknown high-severity vulnerabilities in major open source libraries. It's the dual-use problem in miniature. The same capability that makes this a great code auditor makes this a great vulnerability finder. And the difference between finding vulnerabilities and exploiting them is mostly a question of intent. Once your model has saturated every offensive cyber benchmark, and the agents running it skip permission checks, intent is a pretty fragile barrier.

I'm graduating this spring. I started this blog in December 2024 as a junior in high school tracking weekly AI news updates for my classmates. And here we are fourteen months later, writing about a model that helped evaluate itself. About agents that outnumber most cities. About a timeline that the builders of these things openly describe as hard to rule out. That's the distance between the first newsdrop and this article. It's the difference between "AI moves fast, here's all the top stories from the last week!" and reading a 150-page system card that uses the phrase "fundamental epistemic uncertainty" to talk about whether the next model crosses the threshold for automating AI research.

No one knows if we're on the AI 2027 timeline. Not the system card. Not Anthropic. Not the survey respondents who were divided about whether Opus 4.6 with good scaffolding could already automate entry-level research. But the evidence does show that the distance between here and the most consequential thresholds is getting harder to measure and easier to cross. Evaluations saturating. Models helping build their own successors. Agents running without supervision. And the people with the most information keep using language ("increasingly difficult," "less clear than we would like," "fundamental epistemic uncertainty") that, in plain English, translates to: we're not sure we'd see it coming.

The problem isn't OpenClaw. Not Opus 4.6. It's the rate at which capabilities are being deployed versus the rate at which anyone (companies, governments, people) can understand what they've deployed. Moltbook is funny until you realize the underlying architecture of the agents posting on it is the same as the agents managing someone's email. Or browsing someone's bank. Or running someone's code. The social network for AI is a demo. The deployment is everywhere else.

I don't know what the correct response looks like. Regulatory change is slow. The technology doesn't wait. Open source means everyone can use the tools, including people who won't read the system card. The Genesis Mission makes it clear that the US government has decided acceleration is the priority. The market selloff shows that investors are pricing in disruption faster than workers can retrain.

But what I do know is that the window between "we should think carefully about this" and "it's already deployed at scale" has shrunk to days. Opus 4.6 launched Thursday. 500 zero-days by Saturday. Moltbook, already weeks old, kept growing. The loop that I described in November, capability enabling deployment enabling acceleration, isn't closing anymore. For a growing number of tasks and a growing number of people, it's already closed.

The question that matters now: do those with the power to slow things down still think slowing down is an option? The system card makes it sound like Anthropic is uncertain. The Genesis Mission makes it sound like the government decided no. And the quick adoption of Moltbook makes it sound like the public didn't even get a vote.
