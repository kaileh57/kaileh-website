Fable, Mythos, and the Consequences of a Warning Unheeded

June 26, 2026

On a Tuesday, Anthropic launched the most advanced public model it had. By Friday evening it had been taken down. Now, as I'm writing this two weeks later, it's still down.

On June 9 Anthropic launched Claude Fable 5. On June 12 at 5:21pm Eastern, the Commerce Department sent Anthropic an order. The order directed Anthropic to immediately suspend access to Fable 5 (and its no-holds-barred counterpart, Mythos 5) for all foreign nationals in the world, both inside and outside the United States. Even its own employees if they aren't US citizens.

Anthropic couldn't instantaneously check the citizenship of every API request, so the only thing it could do was take both models down for everyone (including US citizens). It's the first time the US government has used export controls to pull a software model off the market instead of going after the hardware. The precedent is the key here. The model is almost secondary.

To understand why it's happening, it's key to understand what Fable and Mythos really are. They're the same. They're just wearing different masks. Fable and Mythos are built on the same set of underlying weights.

Mythos 5 is the unadulterated model. It has the most powerful offensive cyber and life sciences capabilities of any model Anthropic has made. It's only available to a select few vetted partners via an initiative called Project Glasswing.

Fable 5 is that same model, with safety classifiers applied. If you ask about anything in cybersecurity, biology, chemistry or distillation, it forwards the request to the less powerful Opus 4.8 model. The numbers are striking. Fable scores 80.3% on SWE-Bench Pro. Opus 4.8 gets 69.2%. GPT-5.5 gets 58.6%. Mythos 5 scores 78% on ExploitBench. Opus 4.8 gets only 40.

Anthropic implemented these safeguards on Fable precisely because it felt the underlying model was dangerous enough to need them. It said so loudly and emphatically in every announcement. This matters more than anything else here.

The official trigger for the ban was a jailbreak. Anthropic says in its own account that it was notified orally by officials that someone had found a way to get around Fable's safety measures. When Anthropic examined what it believes was the actual demo, it was basically a way to prompt the model to look at a certain codebase and fix the software vulnerabilities it found. This exposed a few minor, previously known issues.

Their argument is simple and at first glance persuasive: it's not a universal jailbreak, unleashing the full cyber arsenal of the model, but a narrow one. It gets nothing that defenders don't do on a regular basis. The same prompt gets similar behavior from other publicly available models (e.g. OpenAI's GPT-5.5), which faced no such directive.

Anthropic was emphatic: if a single narrow jailbreak was reason enough to recall a model serving hundreds of millions of users, that reasoning could be used to stop any frontier model in the industry.

But then things get murkier. There are at least three competing versions of how the government found out about this, and they don't flatter the same parties.

As reported by Semafor, the White House was notified by Amazon's CEO Andy Jassy, whose company is one of the largest investors in Anthropic. Jassy reportedly also passed along a second, more serious claim: that a group tied to China may have gained access to Mythos.

David Sacks, AI advisor to the administration, has framed it as Anthropic being warned and deciding to do nothing. Anthropic's version is that it got verbal evidence of something benign or inconsequential, and no disclosure of any harmful outcome whatsoever.

Politico's account of the weekend describes a flurry of tense exchanges between Amodei, National Cyber Director Sean Cairncross, Treasury Secretary Bessent, and Commerce Secretary Lutnick, with officials holding firm. A senior White House official said the export controls were a last resort after trying for hours to get the company's cooperation.

This could be a safety system working as intended, or it could be a grudge seizing on a pretext. The reporting supports either. Which is itself a problem.

But then, three days after the ban, the story took a completely different turn with a leak that reframed the whole incident. It was about something much bigger than a prompt for reading codebases.

On June 14, The Economist reported that Senator Mark Warner, vice chair of the Senate Intelligence Committee, had passed on information from General Joshua Rudd, who is in charge of both the NSA and US Cyber Command. Rudd shared it during a briefing on June 11 (a day before the directive was issued).

General Rudd reportedly said that in an authorized red team exercise, Mythos had penetrated "almost all of our classified systems, not in weeks, but in hours." Want to understand why the government has shown no urgency at all about restoring access? Here's your sentence. It completely recasts the export control, from an argument over a narrow API jailbreak to a struggle against an offensive cyber weapon with autonomous capabilities. That weapon had just breezed through the front door of the agency whose sole purpose is to stop this from happening.

Big claim, with nothing to back it up other than a single quote, secondhand, that has since been partly walked back by the person who published it. Within days, Shashank Joshi, the editor at The Economist responsible for the piece, said that line shouldn't be taken literally. The exercise almost certainly involved Mythos working alongside other tools in very specific simulated conditions. He said he had made a mistake in leaving out those caveats.

A US official went even further, saying Warner had misunderstood Rudd. The "hours, not weeks" phrasing was real, but it was about a red teaming effort. It was the NSA testing its own defenses with the model, not an adversary unleashing the model on classified infrastructure.

There's no incident report. No CISA or NSA technical bulletin. No disclosure of methodology or scope. No independent corroboration of any kind. Just a single quote. It went from a classified briefing, to a senator, to a magazine, to every timeline in existence. It grew in certainty with every step but gained no new facts.

The cybersecurity researchers who pushed back on Anthropic's initial marketing of Mythos also have a point worth noting here. The company's headline claim, thousands of severe zero-days, reportedly rested on a couple hundred manual reviews. A model that's great at finding bugs is not the same as a model that outmaneuvered the NSA all by itself. And the gap between them is right where you find this kind of story.

So pick your version, whichever feels more likely. Nobody outside that briefing room can verify the thing that did the most to justify the ban. The most consequential AI policy move the US has ever made rests partly on a quote its own author says shouldn't be taken at face value. This should worry you, regardless of whether you think Mythos is dangerous or not. It probably is. But that's a separate question from whether the process that got rid of it was sound.

And this is where the irony stops being incidental and starts to look structural. Two days before the directive, on June 10, Dario Amodei published a post titled "Policy on the AI Exponential." In it, he singled out his own model, Mythos, as the quintessential example of the national security threat that frontier systems now pose.

He called for mandatory third-party testing of frontier models in cybersecurity, bioweapons, loss of control, and automated R&D. He argued that the government should have the power to block unsafe deployments through a transparent, fair, technically grounded process.

Just days earlier, the company had asked the whole industry to agree on a coordinated brake on frontier development. For months Anthropic had been telling Washington, in press release after press release, that these models were powerful enough to be treated as weapons. Someone in charge should be able to stop a dangerous one from being released.

And then someone in charge did exactly that, to this exact model. Anthropic's objection was that the process wasn't transparent. It wasn't fair. It had no technical grounding. Cyber researcher Peter Girnus put it about as well as it can be put. The company, he said, wrote the legal predicate itself and labeled it a brand. If you spend two years talking about how your product is a munition, don't be surprised when a government decides to regulate it as one.

The clearest way to tell how much of this is about the model and how much is about the relationship is to look at what happened with OpenAI in the same week. On June 25, OpenAI agreed to roll out GPT-5.6 in stages. Both OpenAI and the administration reportedly consider this model comparable to Mythos in cybersecurity.

The arrangement: a limited preview for enterprise partners, with the government approving access customer by customer. The request came from the Office of the National Cyber Director and the Office of Science and Technology Policy. The same group of officials. And Lutnick, the same Commerce Secretary who signed the export order for Anthropic, advised OpenAI not to launch without cross-agency approval.

This is the difference between a negotiated rollout and a kill switch. Same week. Same agencies. Similar capability. And one company gets a collaborative ramp, while the other has its model suddenly taken offline around the world with ninety minutes' notice. Meanwhile, Altman told his staff that this customer-by-customer approval isn't what OpenAI wants in the long run. That's a remarkable thing to be mildly annoyed about while your competitor is completely dark.

The backstory is key to reading the asymmetry. In late February, the Pentagon designated Anthropic a supply-chain risk, a label usually reserved for foreign adversary contractors. That happened after the company refused to let Claude be used for fully autonomous weapons or mass domestic surveillance.

Trump ordered federal agencies to stop using its tools. Within hours of that blacklisting, OpenAI announced a deal to put its models on the Pentagon's classified networks. Anthropic sued the administration in March. It won a preliminary injunction restoring its federal work, lost a separate bid at the appeals court, and is still in active litigation.

So, the company that got the export-control hammer is the one suing the government over its safety guardrails. The company that got the gentle, staggered preview is the one that struck a deal as soon as the first company got sanctioned. The capabilities may be similar, but the postures toward the administration could not be more different. The treatment is in line with the posture, not the benchmark.

Trump told Axios last week that he no longer sees Anthropic as a national security threat. If you take that at face value, the company's threat level is now dictated by how the president is feeling, not by anything in a system card.

The collateral damage points in the same direction. The order didn't just lock out adversaries. It locked out the Five Eyes. The UK's AI Security Institute (the single most important international body for evaluating frontier models for safety) was cut off from systems it was in the middle of testing. An export control justified by safety just cut off the world's top safety evaluator from the model it was trying to make safe.

Allied banks and agencies that had built on Mythos through Glasswing lost access without warning. Former British security minister Tom Tugendhat's reaction was that after a lesson this clear, every nation will start asking what it needs to achieve sovereignty. That's exactly the outcome US AI policy claims to be trying to prevent.

Gary Marcus pointed out the deeper incoherence. An administration that says it must beat China at AI just gave every Chinese-born researcher at a US lab a fresh reason to go back home. It gave every foreign government another reason to distrust American AI as a dependency. You can loosen chip exports to China and hard-block allied access to a model in the same month only if you're making policy one weekend at a time.

I've written before about the loop closing: capability outrunning evaluation, evaluation outrunning oversight, oversight outrunning the public. The Fable episode is what it looks like when the government finally reaches into that loop and grabs something.

The optimistic take is that a brake now demonstrably exists. The state can pull a deployed frontier model off the market in an afternoon. This is the brake Anthropic and many safety-minded people have been asking for.

The pessimistic take is that this brake was pulled on a verbal, unverified basis. It was pulled on one company and not its near-identical competitor, justified by a quote its own author retracted, with no statute, no published evidence, and no process that could be replicated or appealed. Both takes are simultaneously true.

We've learned that the off switch is real. And we've learned that no one can tell you the rule for when it gets used, because there isn't one yet. There's an executive order calling for a pre-release review framework that wasn't even up and running when Fable shipped seven days after it.

This is the field I'm about to enter. I'm starting CS this fall, and the work I want to do, autonomous systems and the perception and ML stack that undergirds them, falls squarely in the cybersecurity and dual-use categories that just got a model wiped from the internet by letter.

The lesson I'm taking from these fourteen days is not that the government was right or wrong. I genuinely can't tell that from the outside, and neither can anyone else who wasn't in that briefing.

The lesson is that the rules for releasing the most capable software people have ever built are being written right now, in tense phone calls over a weekend, based on claims no one can verify. Anthropic asked for a world where someone could stop a dangerous model from being released. And it got that world. It just didn't get to be the one holding the brake.
